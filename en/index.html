Who Holds the Match, Who Becomes the Firewood

💡 Out of laziness, I let this blog lapse for the past two months; updates now resume. This post reflects things as they stood on December 20, 2025. It is the season of China’s national postgraduate entrance exam again. The campus is packed with people who have come from all over, their faces marked by the same kind of weariness. Last year, I was one of them. In this cycle of economic downturn, scarce jobs and shrinking opportunity, the scene carries a silent tragedy. When the exam is no longer a pursuit of the intellect but merely a short-term buffer against reality; when what waits at the end is no longer decent work but another encounter with the very predicament one was forced to flee — then this struggle, waged in the name of “reaching the shore” (as passing is called), becomes nothing more than a carefully named form of suffering.

The Price of Becoming Myself

There is a certain kind of person. They see through the rules by which the world runs earlier than the rest of us, and move through the power fields of human relations with an almost instinctive polish. They know how to present themselves, they excel at socializing, and they calibrate every interaction as an investment in future resources. I have to admit that I envy them. The envy comes from a deep comparison with myself. I was shaped in a world of high certainty, where the rules were clear, the path was singular and evaluation was objective. The logic of solving exam problems became my instinct: rely on ability, believe in open competition, trust that the judgment of worth should in the end come back to talent and learning themselves. It protected my sincerity and built my rationality. But once I step into the gray areas beyond the rules, this logic shows how fragile it is. I am clumsy at scheming and planning, and too ashamed to actively manage myself as a resource. And so I see them. They have set aside the sense of shame I hold dear, and they move with ease through the very situations in which I hesitate. I envy that capacity to act, that courage to face desire head-on and throw oneself at a goal with everything one has. But the other side of envy is a deeper wariness: wariness of the way such efficiency erodes the pure pursuit of learning, wariness of the arrogance and hypocrisy that may breed beneath such polish.

Inside a Gentle, Transparent Cage

In Psychopolitics, Byung-Chul Han, with his customary coolness and edge, dissects a new form of power that is now emerging. It no longer relies on prohibition and discipline; it has learned to embrace freedom, even to exploit it. This is a shrewd power: instead of bluntly saying no, it lures us, in the upbeat tone of “you can,” into a more efficient system of domination. Han argues that the disciplinary society Foucault described, along with its technologies of power, is already obsolete. In its place stands the achievement society. Here, oppression no longer comes from a clearly identifiable outside; it has been cleverly internalized. Each of us has become our own achievement-subject — at once the master cracking the whip and the slave running ourselves ragged. In the name of self-realization we carry out an endless exploitation of ourselves, and the ultimate beneficiary of this race is not the exhausted individual but invisible capital itself. Hence Han’s diagnosis that the classic model of class struggle no longer works, because it has turned into an inner war with the self. Those who fail no longer blame the system; prompted by the insinuation that “you could have tried harder,” they painfully blame themselves. This exploitation without class distinctions makes it hard for a “we” capable of acting together politically to take shape, and the very ground of revolution is quietly dissolved.

2025.10.16 Reading

Where Souls Are Vaporized

Having finished 1984, I still feel a chill reverberating through my body, laced with vertigo and nausea. It is not fear of the plot’s violence but a deeper cold that reaches the bone — the cold of watching a person’s inner world be taken apart, systematically and calmly, until it evaporates. Its horror is structural: as the plot advances layer by layer, it finally makes you believe that the Winston who is hollowed out until nothing is left but his love for Big Brother was inevitable.

At the Threshold of the Torrent

Today is my seventh day at Shenzhen University. This campus — and Shenzhen behind it — is presenting me with a set of stark paradoxes. SZU has world-class resources: opportunities within arm’s reach, generous funding, a transport network seamlessly stitched into the city. And yet, at the most basic level of everyday experience, it shows a rough edge: canteens that come nowhere near those of Tianjin Normal University, where I did my undergraduate degree; a campus network wildly out of keeping with the reputation of this hub of innovation; and roads made dangerous by the absence of any planned lanes for bikes and scooters.
