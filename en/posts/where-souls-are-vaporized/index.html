Where Souls Are Vaporized

Jethro Vanbrook & Gemini 2.5 ProOctober 16, 2025 Translated with Claude Opus 5.5

Having finished 1984, I still feel a chill reverberating through my body, laced with vertigo and nausea. It is not fear of the plot’s violence but a deeper cold that reaches the bone — the cold of watching a person’s inner world be taken apart, systematically and calmly, until it evaporates. Its horror is structural: as the plot advances layer by layer, it finally makes you believe that the Winston who is hollowed out until nothing is left but his love for Big Brother was inevitable.

The first aim of totalitarianism is to abolish objective reality. In the Ministry of Love, Winston fights for two plus two making four. What he defends is not an arithmetic problem but a self-evident outer world that can be shared with others. That is the bedrock on which we confirm our own existence. Once it is pulled away, once the right to define truth is monopolized by power, a person becomes a drifting island, and the sense of reality collapses with it. The vacuum in the soul can in the end be filled only by power. “He loved Big Brother” is not a lie but the end point of a logic.

More hopeless than the destruction of the individual is the immortal system that O’Brien reveals. The Party pursues power as an end in itself. It has built a structural immortality that transcends bloodlines and individuals. The enemy is not a tyrant who ages and dies, but a self-perpetuating logic of domination, a system of thought that never tires, never errs and is always right. What you rebel against is not a person but a ghostly, non-biological political Cthulhu. Before such a system, any individual sacrifice looks insignificant — a brief burning, doomed to fail.

For most people, the system need not even resort to violence. It only has to grant a harmless freedom: the freedom to float and sink among lotteries, alcohol and cheap entertainment. Freedom of thought is permitted only because thought has long since ceased to exist. This is an art of rule more efficient than the Thought Police: it gets the ruled to censor and castrate themselves. The freedom of the proles is a carefully designed, harmless “freedom,” a cage they might even be grateful for. It is the sorrow of the frog in slowly heating water — the most thorough contempt for the human mind.

In the end, Winston fails. As resolute as he is, he is crushed all the same. That is exactly what Orwell most wanted to tell his readers: this book is not a pessimistic verdict on human nature but a tragic hymn to its fragility. It is precious precisely because it can break. That moment of holding Julia in the room above the shop, that futile insistence on objective truth, those words written in a diary that was always destined for the flames — these things, defenseless before absolute power, are the whole proof of what makes us human. Their value does not depend on the outcome; it lies in their very existence.

To understand 1984 is not to learn how to become an invincible hero, but to understand that we should do everything in our power to protect a world that allows us to be fragile. What we protect is not the self that could survive Room 101, but the possibility of never having to walk into Room 101 at all.
