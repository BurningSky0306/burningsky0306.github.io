Past the Singularity, I Am the Only Bottleneck

Once frontier models cross one’s personal adaptive singularity, the barrier holding back a task retreats from the tool to oneself. Faced with a surplus of compute I cannot spend down, I have had to step out from the center of question-and-answer interaction and try to become the one who designs systems that run on their own.

For the past few months, I have had a token budget that is finite yet more than ample for my own needs. How to put those tokens to full use has become my most pressing preoccupation. The contrast grew sharper still two weeks ago, after I upgraded from Claude Pro to Max. Every day I pour hours into burning through my quota, and every day I manage to spend no more than half.

For a long time, I was used to waiting for smarter models and blaming any stall on the limits of my tools. Yet since the release of Opus 5.5, given the premise that my own capabilities have continued to grow gradually, almost none of my tasks have ground to a halt because a model fell short. For me, the advance of frontier models has reached an “adaptive singularity.” Working with any model at the level of Opus 5.5 or above, across every scenario I work in, whatever blocks or delays a task now lies in me, no longer in the model.

To move a project forward, I have to spend long stretches grilling the model back and forth, working out a detailed plan that I can actually grasp, designing the workflow architecture and testing the runs myself. Across the entire chain of a task, the most time-consuming link is precisely the part where I am involved. If I were to measure personal capacity in terms of Claude subscription tiers — or more precisely, to gauge my understanding, deployment and mastery of AI Agents by the quota I can spend productively, making sure every token pulls its weight rather than spinning in place — then at peak load I amount to at most a Claude Pro 2.5x. Even pouring hours of focused effort into interacting with it each day, I barely touch half of a Max allowance. Once I leave room for rest and a sustainable pace, that figure drops back to Claude Pro 1.7x.

Looking back to the second half of last year, when I spoke of what the rise of AI and AI Agents had given me most, I summed it up under three headings: an advance in outlook, an expansion of capability and self-consistent frameworks. At the time, I was thrilled that the barrier to unfamiliar fields had been compressed to the point where I dared to try my hand at them anytime, thrilled that a thought surfacing at any moment could meet an immediate reply and be carried further, and grateful for how quickly fragmentary knowledge could be marshaled into a coherent schema. In hindsight, though, those three gains satisfied me because I was still standing at the center of the interaction; at bottom, every session was still a “two-person Q&A.” Thought had finally found high-performance, real-time feedback, yet the cadence of that feedback remained tethered to my own physical stamina.

Paging through several blog posts officially published by Anthropic, I realized that frontier researchers had begun trying to build long-running agents / always-on agents quite early on. Their explorations held up a mirror to the real reason I had stalled at “Claude Pro 1.7x”: so long as I insist on reviewing every intermediate step by hand and serving as the trigger for every loop myself, I will forever be feeding exponentially expanding compute with the linear time of my own physical body.

It has dawned on me that when it comes to deepening my understanding, deployment and mastery of AI Agents today, the most urgent task is no longer honing the craft of a single exchange. What I need is to design, as far as possible, a system in which AI Agents run autonomously and iterate on themselves. In this new division of labor, my role is simply to design, evaluate and refine the system, while all the front-line work is carried out by the AI Agents themselves. From my elation last year at having real-time feedback for my thoughts to my present self-examination before an unspent surplus of compute, perhaps I have walked a little further down this road.

And yet a new question rises to meet me: if I were to let AI Agents run through days and nights without me, swallowing in an instant the quotas I could never finish spending, at what level would a system that outruns my own scrutiny be waiting to meet me again — its newly minted bottleneck?
