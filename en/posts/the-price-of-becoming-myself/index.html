The Price of Becoming Myself

Jethro Vanbrook & Gemini 2.5 ProNovember 2, 2025 Translated with Claude Opus 5.5

There is a certain kind of person. They see through the rules by which the world runs earlier than the rest of us, and move through the power fields of human relations with an almost instinctive polish. They know how to present themselves, they excel at socializing, and they calibrate every interaction as an investment in future resources.

I have to admit that I envy them. The envy comes from a deep comparison with myself. I was shaped in a world of high certainty, where the rules were clear, the path was singular and evaluation was objective. The logic of solving exam problems became my instinct: rely on ability, believe in open competition, trust that the judgment of worth should in the end come back to talent and learning themselves. It protected my sincerity and built my rationality. But once I step into the gray areas beyond the rules, this logic shows how fragile it is. I am clumsy at scheming and planning, and too ashamed to actively manage myself as a resource. And so I see them. They have set aside the sense of shame I hold dear, and they move with ease through the very situations in which I hesitate. I envy that capacity to act, that courage to face desire head-on and throw oneself at a goal with everything one has. But the other side of envy is a deeper wariness: wariness of the way such efficiency erodes the pure pursuit of learning, wariness of the arrogance and hypocrisy that may breed beneath such polish.

This is the root of my contradiction: a self shaped by pure reason has collided violently with a world governed by practical reason. What I hold fast to and what I envy point to two entirely different scripts for a life.

This is not a simple moral choice but a fundamental opposition between two logics of survival. On one side is the logic of the transaction. It sees the world as a market, relationships as bargaining chips, the self as a project awaiting the best offer. On the other is the logic of value. It believes that creation comes before exchange, that the depth of a relationship springs from a resonance of minds, that inner order is in itself an achievement.

Foucault pointed out that power is not merely repression but a productive force. It produces knowledge and discourse; it also produces subjects. Tools shape the subject, just as language shapes thought. The ability I am talking about here — that extreme form of instrumental rationality — is precisely a technology of power. It is not a neutral tool but a disciplinary system. In learning and using it, the individual is inevitably permeated and remade by its logic. When people start building their networks in the language of managing up and trading resources, they are not just using a technique; they are being produced by it. Qualities such as sincerity and humility, within this system, are if not useless then costs that drag on efficiency. Instrumental rationality thus completes its corrosion of value rationality.

The choice, then, goes beyond strategy and reaches the level of existence. To choose the transaction is to choose a faster ascent, at the price of a self produced by technologies of power. To choose value is to choose a longer, more winding road — an attempt to keep, within the ubiquitous network of power, a place where a more authentic self can dwell.

Building is slow. But sometimes the price of a choice is flesh and blood itself.
