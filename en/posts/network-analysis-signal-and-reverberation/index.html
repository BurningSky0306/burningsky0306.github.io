Network Analysis: Signal and Reverberation

Jethro Vanbrook & Gemini 2.5 ProOctober 15, 2025 Translated with Claude Opus 5.5

In my undergraduate years, when I learned next to nothing, network analysis was one of the few bodies of knowledge I genuinely put my heart into — and was proud of.

I recently learned that an article published on October 9 in Nature Human Behaviour, Statistical evidence in psychological networks, systematically questions the reliability of the statistical evidence in this field. One key fact: its authors come mainly from the field’s birthplace — the University of Amsterdam in the Netherlands.

As I see it, this article is not a challenge from outside but an act of self-examination from the core. Its real impact may lie not in its technical details — how cautiously the “edges” of a network ought to be interpreted, for instance — but in the signal sent by the act of critique itself.

Within the power structure of academic publishing, when a warning comes from an authoritative source, its content gets simplified while its stance gets amplified. One of journal editors’ core concerns is avoiding risk. They have no need, then, to work out exactly where the line lies for a submission to be good enough; they need only remember one conclusion: “network analysis is risky.” This sets off a systemic aversion to risk, a top-down ratcheting in which every level adds requirements of its own. A mild recommendation of “caution” at the top, passed down to the level of execution, naturally hardens into a stricter threshold for entry — the inevitable dissipation and distortion of power and responsibility as they are handed down. The margin first set aside to guard against error ends up as an extra burden on those at the bottom. The cost of the critique is ultimately passed on to the authors at the very end of the chain. So even a study that perfectly avoids every problem the paper raises may find it systematically harder to publish. What matters is no longer whether you made a mistake, but whether your field has been labeled “high-risk.”

For anyone still sitting on research of this kind, however beautifully it was done, the conclusion is clear: finish it and submit it as soon as you can, before the new, stricter consensus sets in. When the rules are changing, speed itself is an important variable.

As for me, my motives when I first turned to network analysis were not pure: it was mostly an unspoken utilitarianism and an opportunistic laziness. I thought that by taking ready-made data and swapping in a more fashionable analytical method, I could easily carve out a shortcut. Those days of exploration were, in truth, easy and pleasant. Seen from here, the little path on which I once pinned such “high hopes” is growing ever narrower.
