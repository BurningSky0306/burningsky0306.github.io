Inside a Gentle, Transparent Cage

Jethro Vanbrook & Gemini 2.5 ProOctober 18, 2025 Translated with Claude Opus 5.5

In Psychopolitics, Byung-Chul Han, with his customary coolness and edge, dissects a new form of power that is now emerging. It no longer relies on prohibition and discipline; it has learned to embrace freedom, even to exploit it. This is a shrewd power: instead of bluntly saying no, it lures us, in the upbeat tone of “you can,” into a more efficient system of domination. Han argues that the disciplinary society Foucault described, along with its technologies of power, is already obsolete. In its place stands the achievement society. Here, oppression no longer comes from a clearly identifiable outside; it has been cleverly internalized. Each of us has become our own achievement-subject — at once the master cracking the whip and the slave running ourselves ragged. In the name of self-realization we carry out an endless exploitation of ourselves, and the ultimate beneficiary of this race is not the exhausted individual but invisible capital itself. Hence Han’s diagnosis that the classic model of class struggle no longer works, because it has turned into an inner war with the self. Those who fail no longer blame the system; prompted by the insinuation that “you could have tried harder,” they painfully blame themselves. This exploitation without class distinctions makes it hard for a “we” capable of acting together politically to take shape, and the very ground of revolution is quietly dissolved.

Han calls the workings of this power a hell of positivity. With one hand it manufactures burnout and depression; with the other it sells remedies for “healing.” Whether mindfulness meditation or fitness classes, their ultimate purpose is not real liberation but the repair of the achievement machine, so that it can run more efficiently still. This is the cruel logic of “healing as killing”: in the name of positivity, it kills all the negativity in a person — fragile, passive, imperfect, yet real. And in Han’s view, that negativity is precisely the source of vitality.

The digital world is the perfect theater for this power. Han argues that the Orwellian Big Brother of 1984 has been replaced by a “friendly Big Brother.” It no longer imposes surveillance; with likes, follows and personalized recommendations, it coaxes us into exposing ourselves of our own accord. We datafy our own lives and settle into a digital panopticon. We trade our eager sharing for an illusion of connection — an illusion that fills precisely the emotional vacuum left behind when real community crumbled under social atomization.

On this basis Han advances the central thesis of psychopolitics: technologies of power have evolved from a biopolitics that manages bodies into a psychopolitics that intervenes directly in the psyche. With big data as its scalpel, it slips past the rational defenses of consciousness and operates at the pre-reflective level of the unconscious and the emotions. It shapes my desires before I am even aware of them. When behavior can be predicted and choices steered, free will is reduced to an exquisite illusion. Human beings end up alienated into objective entities that can be measured, quantified and manipulated.

Faced with this almost seamless picture of power, Han offers no grand social prescription. At the end of the book he turns to an ancient figure: the idiot. This points not to a lack of intellect but to a stance toward existence — the heretic who chooses to go offline, to fall silent, to take no part in the endless race for achievement. He calls this resistance “idiotism.” To me it looks less like active confrontation than a lucid escape with a tinge of cynicism: a small-scale art of survival for when there is no other choice. By refusing to be quantified and refusing to be drawn into communication, one keeps, in an over-transparent world, an inner space for the self that stays opaque.

Perhaps we cannot wholly become Han’s idiot. But the point of understanding Psychopolitics may be to realize that we can at least keep for ourselves the right to become one. In those moments we deliberately create, when we briefly lose touch with the world, we can slip the shackles of the digital world and reaffirm a human depth that no data can define. And yet, when we who gaze into the abyss choose to close our eyes for a moment — what kind of gaze is that, in itself?
