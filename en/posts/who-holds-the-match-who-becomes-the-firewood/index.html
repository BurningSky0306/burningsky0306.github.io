Who Holds the Match, Who Becomes the Firewood

Jethro Vanbrook & Gemini 2.5 ProJanuary 24, 2026 Translated with Claude Opus 5.5

💡 Out of laziness, I let this blog lapse for the past two months; updates now resume. This post reflects things as they stood on December 20, 2025.

It is the season of China’s national postgraduate entrance exam again. The campus is packed with people who have come from all over, their faces marked by the same kind of weariness. Last year, I was one of them.

In this cycle of economic downturn, scarce jobs and shrinking opportunity, the scene carries a silent tragedy. When the exam is no longer a pursuit of the intellect but merely a short-term buffer against reality; when what waits at the end is no longer decent work but another encounter with the very predicament one was forced to flee — then this struggle, waged in the name of “reaching the shore” (as passing is called), becomes nothing more than a carefully named form of suffering.

It is like a cold meat grinder, and an admission rate of twenty-something percent is its only rule. A million candidates, stripped of any other choice, scramble to squeeze into the machine, while people on the sidelines wave flags and cheer: “Hang in there,” “Work hard,” “You can do it.” And so flesh and pain alike are ground down inch by inch and spat out, giving off two smells: one called “the deprivation of choice,” the other “the deprivation of the right to complain.” The winners have obeyed the law of the jungle at the cost of their subjectivity and dignity; the losers are erased along with their suffering.

To rename forced endurance as voluntary devotion is exactly what Pierre Bourdieu described as the symbolic violence of power against the powerless — a violence exercised upon social agents with their own complicity. Suffering does not produce meaning by itself; meaning should be given by the one who suffers. When structures of oppression are glorified as heroic sacrifice, they hide behind a moving story: the sufferers lose the language to accuse, the onlookers gain a reason to feel at ease, and those who apply the pressure win moral absolution. This is not respect for suffering but one last trampling of dignity. It is an ideological alchemy that turns passive endurance into eager pursuit and refines pain into meaning. It does not deny suffering; it deifies it. It does not suppress resistance; it dissolves the legitimacy of resistance. Its final logic runs: you were crushed not because the machine is merciless, but because you chose it.

What really needs to be distinguished is not whether one burns, but where the flame comes from: which fires answer an inner calling, and which are merely offerings set alight from outside. The precondition for burning by choice is the freedom not to burn. The problem was never the burning itself, but who holds the match, and who is forced to become the firewood.

I have said to others, and to myself: “Keep going, just hold on a little longer.” Now all I want is to repent. Half of those words were complicity; half were a plea for mercy.
